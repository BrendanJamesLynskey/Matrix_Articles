01

A Taxonomy That Has to Be Got Right

Jitter is the deviation of a transition from where it should have been. That definition covers several mechanisms with quite different statistics, and the reason to keep them apart is that they combine differently: bounded quantities add arithmetically, unbounded ones add in quadrature, and mixing the two rules produces a budget that passes on the bench and fails in the field.

Random jitter, unbounded

Thermal and flicker noise in the oscillator and the sampling circuit, converted to timing error. Gaussian, so it has no maximum — quoting a peak-to-peak figure for it is meaningless without stating the error ratio it was measured to. Specified as a standard deviation.

Data-dependent jitter, bounded

The channel's own intersymbol interference moving the zero crossing. A deterministic function of the preceding bits, so it is bounded by the worst pattern, and — crucially — it is removed by an equaliser rather than by a better clock.

Periodic jitter, bounded

A tone from somewhere else on the board: a switching regulator, a neighbouring clock, a fan controller. Bounded by its own amplitude, uncorrelated with the data, and the subject of deck 07.

Duty-cycle distortion, bounded

Rising and falling edges treated differently by a threshold that is not at mid-swing. Bounded, easy to measure, and fixed in the transmitter rather than anywhere else.

The composite quantities are total jitter at a specified error ratio, and the split into a random part and a deterministic part that the next slides rely on.

02

Two Quantities Called Q

Before the arithmetic, a collision of notation worth flagging explicitly, because it genuinely catches people who work across both domains.

Communications Q

The argument of the error function: a signal-to-noise ratio measured in standard deviations, so that the error ratio is $\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}(Q/\sqrt{2})$. Dimensionless, typically between three and eight, and larger is better. This is the Q in every link budget in this series.

Resonator Q

Energy stored divided by energy dissipated per radian: a property of a tuned circuit, typically between ten and several thousand, and larger is also better but means something entirely different.

The two meet in a way that makes the confusion harder rather than easier to avoid. The resonator Q of an inductor-capacitor oscillator's tank sets its phase noise; the phase noise integrates to the random jitter; the random jitter is part of the noise in the denominator of the communications Q. A sentence about a high-Q tank improving Q is not nonsense, but it needs both words defined.

The communications Q required for an error ratio of one in a million million is 7.03, and that number appears throughout this series. Note how slowly it moves: three more decades of error ratio costs less than one unit of Q, which is why link budgets are so sensitive near the target and so forgiving far from it.

03

The Dual-Dirac Convention

The industry's reporting convention replaces whatever the deterministic distribution really is with two impulses separated by the peak-to-peak deterministic jitter, each convolved with the Gaussian random part. Total jitter at a given error ratio is then

$$\mathrm{TJ}(\mathrm{BER}) = \mathrm{DJ}_{pp} + 2\,Q(\mathrm{BER})\, \mathrm{RJ}_{\text{rms}}.$$

It is a convention for reporting rather than a description of the physics. The real deterministic distribution is not two impulses; it is a cluster of possible edge positions set by the data pattern. The model is exact only in the far tails, which is, admittedly, where the budget lives.

Notice the structure of the arithmetic. The deterministic term enters once, whatever the error ratio; the random term is multiplied by fourteen at $10^{-12}$. A design dominated by deterministic jitter behaves quite differently under a tightened error-ratio specification from one dominated by random jitter, and the decomposition tells you which you have.

04

Interactive: The Bathtub Curve

Sweep the sampling instant across the unit interval and plot the resulting error ratio on a logarithmic axis and you get a bathtub: high at both edges where the sampler is close to a transition, and low in the middle. Its two walls are complementary error functions, so on a logarithmic axis their tails are close to straight — which is what makes extrapolation tempting.

error ratio against sampling position $10^{-12}$

The horizontal opening at the target error ratio is the eye width the receiver actually has to work with, and it is always smaller than the unit interval by the total jitter. Everything a clock recovery circuit does is an attempt to place its sampling instant in the middle of that opening and keep it there.

05

Interactive: What Extrapolation Misses

No instrument can measure an error ratio of one in a million million in reasonable time; at twenty-eight gigabaud that is one error roughly every thirty-six seconds, and establishing the number statistically takes hours per point. The universal practice is therefore to measure down to about $10^{-6}$, fit the dual-Dirac model, and extrapolate.

That is sound if the jitter really is one Gaussian plus one bounded term. If there is a second, rarer Gaussian — an intermittent aggressor, a marginal supply, a connector that occasionally misbehaves — the measurement cannot see it and the extrapolation does not include it.

What to do about it

Measure further down than is comfortable, because each decade constrains the tail more. Check that the fitted random jitter is stable as the fit range is extended, since a hidden second component makes it drift. And treat a bathtub whose walls are not straight on a logarithmic axis as evidence rather than as measurement noise — curvature is exactly what a second Gaussian produces.

06

Phase Noise, and the Band You Integrate Over

Random jitter comes from the oscillator, and the oscillator is characterised by its phase noise — the power in a one-hertz bandwidth at a given offset from the carrier, relative to the carrier, in dBc/Hz. Converting it to jitter is an integration:

$$\sigma_t = \frac{1}{2\pi f_0}\sqrt{2\int_{f_1}^{f_2} L(f)\,df}.$$

The limits are the important part, and they are a choice rather than a property of the oscillator. A clock recovery loop tracks slow phase movement and rejects it, so the lower limit belongs to the receiver rather than to the transmitter, and a jitter figure quoted without the band it was integrated over is close to meaningless.

07

Data-Dependent Jitter Is Not Jitter at All

The largest single timing term on a lossy channel is not random and does not come from the clock. It is the channel's own intersymbol interference displacing the zero crossing: the tails of earlier pulses are still present when the current edge happens, so the voltage at which the edge starts is different for every history of preceding bits, and the crossing moves.

This can be computed exactly rather than measured, by enumerating the bit patterns and finding the crossing for each. The model does that on the pulse response of the channel from SerDes_Equalisation.

Why the distinction earns its keep

Because it says what to buy. Data-dependent jitter is removed by equalisation — it is the same intersymbol interference the decision-feedback taps are already cancelling, seen in the time axis instead of the voltage axis. Random jitter is removed by a better oscillator. A budget that lumps them together as "total jitter" cannot tell you which of those two rather different purchases will fix your link.

08

The Clock Recovery Loop Decides What Counts

The receiver does not compare incoming edges against a perfect reference. It recovers a clock from the data itself, with a loop that follows slow phase movement and ignores fast movement. Jitter the loop tracks is harmless, because the sampling instant moves with it; jitter it fails to track is what reaches the sampler.

So the loop's jitter transfer is a low-pass and the error — what the sampler actually sees — is its high-pass complement. Slow wander of the transmitter's clock, which would look alarming on an oscilloscope, is entirely benign; fast jitter is not.

jitter transfer (tracked) error response (seen by the sampler) loop bandwidth

The crossover between the two curves is the loop bandwidth, and it is the single most consequential number in a receiver's timing design. Wide bandwidth tracks more jitter but admits more noise from the data transitions; narrow bandwidth filters better and tracks less. Every clock recovery architecture is a position on that trade.

Interactive: Jitter Tolerance

The compliance test that follows from the previous slide applies a sinusoidal jitter tone at each frequency and asks how much the receiver survives. Only the part the loop fails to track reaches the sampler, so the tolerance is the budget divided by the magnitude of the error response — which rises steeply below the loop bandwidth and flattens above it.
