01

The Question This Deck Asks

Power delivery is a large subject and most of it belongs elsewhere. Regulator topologies, on-package voltage regulation, backside power delivery, deep-trench capacitors and the thermal consequences of all of them are covered in the SoC series, deck 09, from the silicon side. This deck asks a narrower question with a definite answer: by what route does a disturbance on the supply become an error at a receiver, and how much of a link's budget does it consume?

The route has three stages and each is computed here. A current step drawn by the switching logic meets the network's impedance and becomes a voltage ripple. The ripple reaches the transmitter and its phase-locked loop, neither of which is perfectly immune, and becomes jitter and amplitude modulation. That jitter lands in the same budget as the intersymbol interference and crosstalk of the earlier decks.

Stage one

Current demand against network impedance. This is where anti-resonance lives, and where the counter-intuitive result about adding capacitors appears.

Stage two

Supply rejection. How much of the ripple reaches the oscillator's control node and the driver's output stage.

Stage three

Frequency modulation becoming phase modulation becoming jitter, filtered by the loop that is supposed to be correcting it.

02

A Capacitor Is an Inductor, Above Its Resonance

A real capacitor is a capacitance in series with a resistance and an inductance. Below the frequency where the capacitive and inductive reactances cancel, it behaves as a capacitor and its impedance falls; above it, the inductance dominates and the impedance rises again. At the series resonance itself the impedance is just the equivalent series resistance.

That single fact is the whole architecture of a decoupling network. No capacitor is useful across the whole band, so the network is built as a ladder of values, each covering the region where the one below it has given up. And because every capacitor is inductive above its resonance, every pair of adjacent values creates a frequency at which one is inductive and the other still capacitive — which is where they resonate against each other.

Notice how little the resonance moves across four decades of capacitance once the mounting is included. The value sets where the capacitor starts working; the mounting sets where it stops.

03

Mounting Inductance Is the Layout's Contribution

The inductance in the previous table has two parts. The part inside the component is a property of the part, and a better part buys a little. The part outside it — the loop from the pads, down the vias, to the planes and back — is a property of the layout, and above a few hundred megahertz it is usually the larger of the two.

Halving the mounting inductance by doubling the number of via pairs raises the useful frequency of every capacitor in the design by a factor of the square root of two, at the cost of two more holes. That is nearly always cheaper than buying a better capacitor, and it is the reason decoupling layout rules talk about via placement rather than about part numbers.

And why capacitors moved under the package

The shortest possible mounting loop is one that goes straight down into the planes with no trace at all, which is why decoupling migrated first to the underside of the board directly beneath the package, then into the package substrate, and finally onto the die. Each step shortens the loop by roughly an order of magnitude, and each step therefore moves the frequency at which the capacitor stops working up by roughly a factor of three.

05

Interactive: When More Capacitors Make It Worse

Here is the result that makes this worth computing rather than asserting. The intuitive response to a PDN that does not meet its target is to add more capacitors. Adding more of one value lowers the impedance in the band where that value is capacitive — and sharpens the anti-resonance above it, because it lowers the inductance of that branch and raises the quality factor of the parallel resonance with the tier above.

worst anti-resonant peak impedance at 100 MHz

What actually helps

Spreading the values so that adjacent tiers overlap rather than leaving a gap; choosing parts with enough equivalent series resistance to damp the resonance rather than the lowest available; and reducing mounting inductance, which moves every resonance up together instead of creating a new one. The instinct to specify the lowest-resistance capacitor available is precisely wrong here: some loss in the branch is what keeps the peak finite.

06

Target Impedance, and What the Rule Is Worth

The standard design target is

$$Z_{\text{target}} = \frac{V_{\text{rail}} \times \text{ripple allowed}} {I_{\text{transient}}},$$

and for a nine-hundred-millivolt rail with five per cent ripple and a twenty-amp transient it gives forty-five milliohms. It is a useful first cut and a poor sign-off criterion, for a reason worth being explicit about: it is a flat number applied to a network whose impedance is anything but flat, driven by a current whose spectrum is anything but white.

Meeting it everywhere is neither necessary nor sufficient. Not necessary, because a peak at a frequency the load never excites costs nothing. Not sufficient, because a network that meets it at every frequency can still fail on a transient whose spectrum happens to concentrate at the one peak that just scrapes under. The honest version of the calculation is done in the time domain against the actual current profile, and the target impedance is what you use before you have one.

07

Simultaneous Switching Noise

When many drivers switch at once, their currents all flow through whatever inductance the supply and return paths share, and the voltage that develops across it is $L\,di/dt$ summed over all of them. The scaling with driver count is what makes this an interface problem rather than a component one: a single driver is harmless and thirty-two switching in step are not.

supply disturbance

This is the strongest argument for the differential signalling of deck 05 and for the line codes that keep the transmitted stream balanced. A differential pair draws a constant current from the supply whichever way it is driven, so its contribution to this sum is close to zero — which is a benefit of differential signalling that is rarely listed and is frequently the largest one on a wide interface.

08

Interactive: From Supply Ripple to Jitter

The last stage of the route. A tone on the supply reaches the oscillator's control node attenuated by the supply rejection of whatever regulates it, and modulates the oscillator's frequency. Frequency modulation integrates to phase modulation, so the phase deviation is the frequency deviation divided by the modulation frequency, and the jitter is that phase divided by the carrier's angular frequency.

The loop itself filters. Inside its bandwidth the feedback corrects the disturbance; outside it, the oscillator is on its own. The consequence is that supply tones just above the loop bandwidth are the dangerous ones, and where the switching regulator's fundamental sits relative to that bandwidth is a design decision rather than an accident.

09

Where the Regulator's Switching Frequency Should Go

The previous slide gives a design rule that is easy to state and frequently ignored. A switching regulator puts a large, narrow, entirely predictable tone on the rail at its switching frequency. That tone becomes periodic jitter in proportion to how poorly the phase-locked loop rejects disturbances at that frequency — and the loop's rejection is worst just above its own bandwidth.

Well below the loop bandwidth

The loop tracks and corrects it. A one-megahertz regulator under a four-megahertz loop contributes very little. This is the comfortable case and it is why low-frequency regulators were never a jitter problem.

Just above it

The worst place. The loop no longer corrects and the modulation index is still large because the tone is at a low enough frequency that a given frequency deviation integrates to a large phase deviation. This is where modern multi-megahertz regulators land by default.

Far above it

Also acceptable, because the phase deviation falls as one over the modulation frequency even though the loop does nothing. Very high switching frequencies are benign for this reason, whatever else they cost.

The practical answer

Pick the regulator's frequency deliberately against the transceiver's loop bandwidth, rather than letting the power team choose it for efficiency and the SerDes team discover it in the jitter decomposition. Spread-spectrum modulation, which smears the tone, makes the peak smaller and the total no better.
